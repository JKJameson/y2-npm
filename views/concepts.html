Key Concepts

A few core ideas come up again and again in trading. This page breaks them down, from valuing a company to reading a chart.

Diversification

Diversification means spreading your money across multiple investments so that a single bad performer can't wipe out your portfolio. The old saying is: "Don't put all your eggs in one basket."

❌ Not Diversified

  • 100% in one stock
  • If that company fails, you lose everything
  • Highly exposed to sector-specific downturns

✓ Diversified

  • Spread across tech, healthcare, energy, etc.
  • One bad stock has limited impact
  • Smoother overall returns over time
Index funds (like those tracking the S&P 500) give you instant diversification across 500 companies in a single purchase. That's why they're popular with beginners.

The P/E Ratio

The Price-to-Earnings ratio (P/E ratio) is one of the most widely used tools for valuing a stock. It tells you how much investors are willing to pay for each euro/dollar of a company's earnings.

Share Price ÷ Earnings Per Share = P/E Ratio
P/E Range What it might mean Typical for
Under 10 Potentially undervalued, or the market expects decline Mature / struggling companies
10 – 20 Fairly valued, in line with average market expectations Established blue-chip companies
20 – 40 Growth premium; investors expect strong future earnings Growing tech companies
40+ Expensive or speculative, with high expectations baked in High-growth startups, AI stocks
⚠ P/E ratio alone doesn't tell the whole story. Always compare against the company's own history and its industry peers.

Dividends

Dividend Yield

The dividend yield shows what percentage return you get from dividends relative to the share price:

Annual Dividend ÷ Share Price × 100 = Yield %

For example, a stock priced at €50 that pays €2 per year in dividends has a yield of 4%.

Dividend Reinvestment Plans (DRIPs)

Many brokers let you automatically reinvest dividends to buy more shares instead of taking the cash. Over time, this compounding effect can significantly grow your position without any extra money out of your own pocket.

Reading Candlestick Charts

A candlestick chart is the most common way to display stock price movement over time. Each "candle" represents one time period (a minute, an hour, a day, etc.) and shows four key prices:

Bullish (Green)

Price closed higher than it opened. The body spans open to close.

Bearish (Red)

Price closed lower than it opened. Red signals a down period.

High

The highest price reached during the period, shown by the top of the wick.

Open

The price at the start of the period.

Close

The price at the end of the period. Close vs. open determines candle colour.

Low

The lowest price reached, shown by the bottom of the wick.

Quick Glossary

New to the jargon? Here are the terms you'll encounter most often:

Bull Market
A period when stock prices are rising or expected to rise. Investor confidence is high.
Bear Market
A period when stock prices fall 20% or more from recent highs. Often accompanied by pessimism.
Portfolio
The total collection of investments held by an individual or fund.
Volatility
How much and how quickly a stock's price moves. High volatility = bigger swings, more risk.
Market Cap
The total market value of a company's shares. Share Price × Total Shares = Market Cap.
Liquidity
How easily a stock can be bought or sold without affecting its price. High volume = high liquidity.
Earnings Per Share (EPS)
A company's profit divided by the number of shares. Higher EPS generally signals a healthier company.
IPO
Initial Public Offering. When a private company sells shares to the public for the first time.
Blue-Chip Stock
Shares in a large, well-established, financially stable company with a long track record.
Short Selling
Borrowing shares to sell now, with the plan to buy them back cheaper later and pocket the difference.
